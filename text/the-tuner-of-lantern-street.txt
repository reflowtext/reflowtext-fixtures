THE TUNER OF LANTERN STREET

A short story


I.

Every town on the estuary had a lighthouse once, and every lighthouse had a
keeper, and when the keepers were let go the towns kept the towers out of
pride. Ostmark kept its tower and filled it with pianos.

Nobody planned that. A school closed and its upright went into the tower's
ground floor because the room was dry. A widower on the hill died and his
nephew, who lived far inland and did not want a grand piano, paid a carter to
take it "anywhere it will be played". The carter knew the tower. After that it
became a thing people did: when a piano had no home, it went to the light.

By the year this story begins there were thirty-one of them, stacked on every
landing of the spiral, and one tuner, whose name was Edda Varn.


II.

Edda had come to Ostmark to tune a single instrument for a single concert and
had stayed eleven years. She told people it was the air. Salt air is murder on
a piano: it swells the soundboard, rusts the strings, makes the felts go soft
and sullen. A tuner in a salt town is never short of work. That was the
practical reason, and she gave it freely.

The other reason she gave to no one. On her first night, after the concert,
she had climbed the tower to look at the water, and on the seventh landing she
had found a small square piano, very old, with a lid painted in faded green
and gold. She had lifted the lid and played one note, a low F, and the note
had come back to her wrong. Not out of tune. Wrong in a way she had no word
for: as if it had been played somewhere else first, a long time ago, and she
was only hearing the end of it.

She had tuned that piano every month since. It never drifted. That was the
other reason.


III.

Her rounds went upward. She started each first Monday on the ground floor
with the school upright, which was always flat and always forgiving, and
climbed one landing a day, so that by the end of the month she reached the
lantern room, where the widower's grand stood under the great dead lens with
its legs chained to the floor against the wind.

She kept a ledger. Each piano had a page, and each visit a line: the date, the
pitch she found, the pitch she left, what she had replaced. The ledger was the
only thing she owned that she would have carried out of a fire. Reading it
back, she could see the whole town's weather in the columns: the wet spring
three years ago when every instrument sank a quarter tone in a fortnight; the
dry August when the grand split a bridge.

The green piano's page was different. Every line said the same thing.

    F, found true. Left true. Nothing replaced.

One hundred and thirty-two lines, all alike, in a hand that grew steadily
smaller as she ran out of patience with herself for writing it.


IV.

In the twelfth year a boy started following her up the stairs.

He was nine or ten, thin, with a cough that came and went with the fog. He
did not say anything. He sat two steps below whatever landing she was working
on and listened. When she struck a fork and held it against the case, he
leaned forward. When she pulled a string up to pitch and the beats between it
and its neighbour slowed and slowed and stopped, he let out his breath at the
same moment she did.

On the fourth day she said, without turning round, "You can hear it."

"Hear what?"

"The beating. When two strings are almost the same note they fight. You hear
it as a wobble. Wah, wah, wah. When they agree, it stops."

"It sounds like someone breathing," the boy said. "And then they hold their
breath."

She did turn round then. Nobody had ever described it to her like that, and
it was exactly right.

His name was Tobin. His mother cleaned the ferry office. He was supposed to be
at school, and he was not, and Edda decided that was not her business.


V.

She taught him the way she had been taught, which is to say badly at first and
then, when she noticed she was doing it badly, slowly.

She taught him to listen before touching anything. To play the octave first,
because an octave is the easiest truth to hear. To set one string and tune the
others against it, never against your own memory of what a note ought to be,
because memory drifts worse than any piano. She taught him that a tuning
hammer is not a wrench, and that the pin wants to be persuaded, not forced:
a little over, then settle it back.

He learned faster than she had. His hands were too small for the heavy pins
on the grand, but on the uprights he could do a unison by himself by the end
of the autumn, and he would look up at her afterwards with an expression she
remembered from somewhere and eventually placed: it was her own face, in a
mirror, at nineteen.

She did not take him to the seventh landing. She told herself it was because
the stairs there were bad.


VI.

The storm came in the second week of December, a long one, three days of wind
from the north-east that pushed the sea up the estuary and over the quay.
Nobody went out. On the third night the power failed across the town, and in
the dark Edda heard, from her room on Lantern Street, the tower ringing.

Not the bell; there had been no bell for forty years. Strings. Thirty-one
pianos, every one of them shaken by the wind through the broken windows of the
upper floors, every string sounding a little, all at once. It was not music.
It was the sound of a building full of held breath.

She dressed and went out into it.

The tower door was open. Tobin was on the stairs with a lantern from the
ferry office, soaked to the skin. He said he had heard it too. He said he did
not want them to be frightened. She did not laugh at him, because it was
exactly what she had been thinking.

They climbed together, closing what shutters they could. On the seventh
landing, the green piano was silent. Everything else in the tower was ringing,
and the green piano sat in the middle of it with its lid down, perfectly
still, as if it were listening.

Tobin went to it before she could stop him. He lifted the lid and played the
low F.


VII.

Afterwards they never agreed on what happened next, and after a while they
stopped trying.

Edda said the note went out into the stairwell and the other pianos answered
it, one by one, the way strings will when a matching pitch reaches them: the
school upright, the widower's grand, all the rest, each finding its own F and
sounding it back. That this was physics, and nothing else. That sympathetic
resonance is taught in the first month of any apprenticeship.

Tobin said that wasn't it. He said the other pianos stopped. That the wind was
still screaming through the top of the tower and all thirty strings-full of
noise just stopped, the way the beating stops when two strings agree, and for
the length of one long breath the only sound in the building was the F.

What they both agreed on was that it held. Long after the note should have
died, it was still there, under the storm, low and patient. And then the
power came back on across the estuary, and the street lamps on the quay
flickered and lit, and when they looked back at the piano the note was gone
and the wind was only wind.


VIII.

The next morning Edda opened the ledger to the green piano's page and wrote
the date. She sat for a long time. Then she wrote:

    F, found true. Left true. Played by T.

She closed the book and gave it to him.

"You'll need to start your own page for it," she said. "Mine are full."

He looked at the ledger, then at her. "Where are you going?"

"Nowhere. Upstairs. The grand split another bridge in the storm and I'll be a
week on it." She stood, and her knees told her how many stairs she had
climbed in twelve years. "But you'll do the seventh landing from now on. It
likes you."

He nodded, as though that were a perfectly ordinary thing for a piano to do.


IX.

People in Ostmark still take their pianos to the light. There are forty-four
of them now. The tuner is a tall man with a cough that comes and goes with
the fog, and he keeps a ledger, and if you ask him nicely he will tell you
that the beating between two strings sounds like someone breathing, and that
when they agree, it stops.

On the seventh landing, if you are quiet, he will let you play one note.

Most people say it sounds perfectly ordinary. A few go very still, and play
it again, and look at him. Those are the ones he teaches.
